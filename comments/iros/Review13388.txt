Reviewer 2 of IROS 2026 submission 3013

Comments to the author
======================

The paper tackles an important problem and provides a
clear, implementable extension of SafeDAgger with a
conservative Q-based risk predictor, supported by solid but
not exhaustive experiments. However, the conceptual advance
is modest, safety claims are confined to simulation, and
the connection between the formal objective and the actual
algorithm is weak.

Weakness 1. Limited conceptual novelty relative to
SafeDAgger and existing safe IL / safe RL work.

The core method is essentially SafeDAgger with an extra
value-based risk gate. The intervention policy is a simple
OR over disagreement and a value threshold (Eq. (6)), and
the risk predictor itself is a rather standard
double-critic Q-estimator trained with SARSA. The paper
does not convincingly argue why this combination is
fundamentally new beyond “we plugged a Q-predictor into
SafeDAgger for dexterous manipulation,” and it does not
deeply compare to other risk/uncertainty-gated interactive
IL variants like ThriftyDagger and LazyDagger (which are
cited in Sec. II-B but only lightly discussed in the
context of human supervision).

The concept of using a learned critic or predicted failure
probability to gate expert queries has appeared in various
forms in safe IL and constrained RL. The paper does not
clearly separate what is genuinely new (e.g., specific
design choices for the risk target in Eq. (5) tailored to
lift success and unsafe events) from what is fairly
standard.

Weakness 2. Formalization in Eq. (1) is not actually
optimized and does not match the algorithm.
The objective in Eq. (1) jointly optimizes over both the
student policy (\pi_S) and the intervention mechanism
(\mathcal{G}), trading off expected success and unsafe
events under (p_{\pi_\mathcal{G}}). In practice,
(\mathcal{G}) is not learned at all: it is hand-crafted as
the OR-rule in Eq. (6), with thresholds (\tau_D) and
(\tau_{\text{success}}) fixed from warm-start statistics.

Weakness 3. Real-world experiments are too limited and lack
baseline comparisons.
Table V reports only the performance of the proposed
pipeline's student on six objects (4 seen, 2 unseen) over
15 episodes each. There are no real-world baselines such
as:

- A student distilled with vanilla DAgger or
disagreement-only SafeDAgger,
- A purely RL-based vision policy trained with domain
randomization,
- Or even ablations of the risk predictor off vs on in the
final deployed policy.

Without any such comparison, it is impossible to discern
whether the safety-aware distillation pipeline is
particularly advantageous in the real world versus
alternative training schemes. The observed higher unsafe
rates on unseen objects (Plane/Shoe; 40% unsafe) may be due
to contact-design issues unrelated to the intervention
mechanism.

Comments on the Video Attachment
================================

It is highly intuitive, with the key points clearly
organised and presented in a logical sequence. The intended
message comes across clearly.
