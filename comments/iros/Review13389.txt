Reviewer 3 of IROS 2026 submission 3013

Comments to the author
======================

This paper proposes a safety-aware policy distillation
framework for dexterous manipulation that integrates
teacher intervention during online interactive imitation
learning. The key idea is to trigger teacher takeover based
on a combination of teacher–student action disagreement and
a learned Q-value-based risk predictor. The method aims to
reduce unsafe exploration during distillation while
maintaining task performance when transferring from
privileged-state RL teachers to RGB-based student policies.


Strengths
-Well-motivated problem setting. 
The paper addresses an important and practical challenge:
reducing unsafe exploration during policy distillation in
dexterous manipulation. This is particularly relevant since
failure modes in dexterous hands can directly translate
into hardware risks.

-Reasonable design intuition.
The combination of action disagreement and a learned risk
predictor is a natural extension of existing
SafeDAgger-style methods. In particular, augmenting
disagreement-based gating with a risk estimate to capture
downstream failures (that are not detectable from local
action similarity) is a sensible and well-motivated idea.

-Autonomous teacher relabeling in an interactive setting.
The method performs fully autonomous expert relabeling
during online distillation using an RL-trained teacher,
without requiring human supervision. This is a meaningful
step in applying interactive imitation learning to
dexterous manipulation.

Weaknesses
- Limited novelty.
The proposed method largely combines existing ideas from
interactive imitation learning and value-based risk
estimation (e.g., ThriftyDAgger), and applies them to new
domain(dexterous manipulation) with a modified RL teacher.
While the integration is reasonable, the overall
contribution appears incremental rather than introducing a
fundamentally new methodology.

- Ambiguity in risk predictor formulation.
The so-called “Q-value-based failure prediction” is trained
using predefined failure labels and bootstrapped
targets, making it closer to a failure probability
estimator than a true action-value function. As such, the
terminology may be somewhat overstated, and its connection
to actual failure likelihood is not rigorously justified.

-Heuristic and fixed threshold design.
The intervention threshold is determined from a warm-start
dataset and kept fixed throughout training, despite the
risk predictor being continuously updated and the data
distribution shifting. The lack of adaptive thresholding or
sensitivity analysis raises concerns about robustness and
general applicability. Additionally, it raises concerns:
Does a fixed threshold remain valid under distribution
shift? No ablation or analysis is provided for threshold
sensitivity, which weakens the methodological
justification.

-Unclear contribution of the risk predictor.
From the results (e.g., Fig. 3), the method maintains a
relatively high intervention rate even in later training
stages, suggesting that improved safety may come from
increased teacher intervention rather than better risk
prediction. Without a fair comparison such as equal
takeover rates, its true benefit remains unclear.

-Experimental limitations and limited generalization.
Although the paper mentions evaluation on multiple objects
(10), detailed results in Tables III/IV and Fig. 4 are
shown for only a subset. Additionally, the number of seeds
or runs is not specified, making it difficult to assess
variance and statistical significance. The method shows a
significant drop in performance on unseen objects, with
unsafe rates rising to around 40%. This indicates that
safety and robustness do not generalize well beyond the
training distribution, limiting practical deployment
claims.
